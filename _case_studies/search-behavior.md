- **Role:** Project Lead / UX Researcher  
- **Skills:** Contextual Inquiry, Information Behavior, Research Synthesis, Stakeholder Facilitation  

## Context
In 2020, Adobe’s Digital Video & Audio (DVA) team wanted to better understand how creators find media assets and use in-app search tools across Premiere Pro, After Effects, Audition, Rush, and Character Animator.  

The machine learning engineers sponsoring the study needed to prioritize new “smart search” and automation features—but they also lacked a clear picture of how creators actually searched and what they expected from search tools.  

As someone with a background in information architecture, I framed the study to serve both tactical and strategic goals: deliver input for immediate feature decisions while uncovering the deeper patterns in users’ information-seeking behavior.

## Goals
We wanted to answer three key questions:
1. What kinds of files or assets are creators looking for?  
2. What are they looking for *within* those assets?  
3. What do they do once they’ve found what they need?  

These questions helped us explore how users think about metadata, tagging, and discovery—and what tools or workarounds they rely on today.

## Methods
- **Participants:** 17 total (creative professionals and hobbyists)  
- **Tools studied:** Premiere Pro, After Effects, Audition, Rush, Character Animator  
- **Format:** Fully remote contextual inquiry sessions  
- **Activities:**  
  - On-screen observation of real search behaviors  
  - Open-ended discussion of workflows  
  - Miro-based force-ranking of desired search features  

I led recruiting (via social media and Reddit groups), facilitated all sessions, and conducted synthesis through thematic clustering and cross-app comparison.

A mid-study check-in with stakeholders revealed richer insights in the Premiere Pro and After Effects cohorts, so we pivoted to focus there—an example of agile study design improving both efficiency and depth.

## Key Findings

### 1. Most asset searches happen outside Adobe apps
Creators rely on Finder or Explorer rather than in-app search. They use folder naming conventions, dates, and thumbnails instead of tags or metadata.

> “Anything that kills a repetitive task is more important than a shiny feature.” — *Yoni, Motion Designer*

### 2. In-app search tools fail basic expectations
Search boxes were often overlooked, unclear in scope, and lacked filtering or sorting. Even long-time users were unaware of features already available.

### 3. Search needs follow predictable cognitive patterns
Creators fell into distinct “information-seeking” modes—from knowing exactly what they needed to not realizing a feature existed at all. These scenarios mapped directly to opportunities for taxonomy and semantic search improvements.

### 4. Creators want automation for tedious editing
The top-ranked requests centered on saving time: removing filler words, isolating speakers, and automatically syncing beats or transitions.

### 5. Discovery remains a persistent challenge
Pop-ups and feature announcements were rarely remembered. Just-in-time aids like rich tooltips or visual cues worked far better.

## Recommendations
- Improve **discoverability** through contextual, visual guidance.  
- Support **semantic search** and maintain controlled vocabularies and synonyms.  
- Add **faceted filtering and sorting** to search interfaces.  
- Prioritize automation that reduces repetitive tasks.  
- Preserve **human help channels** for complex or ambiguous queries.

## Impact
The research gave Adobe’s DVA and ML teams a grounded understanding of how professionals and hobbyists search for and within their assets.  

Findings directly informed feature prioritization and helped validate automation features that later shipped in Premiere Pro—most notably, the **automatic filler word removal** tool that was a top-ranked user request.

## Reflection
This project reinforced how foundational research can meet both short-term and long-term needs. While the immediate goal was helping engineers prioritize features, the broader outcome was a shared mental model of how creators think about finding and organizing their own work.  

It also highlighted how UX research and information architecture intersect—especially when designing tools for managing creative information.
